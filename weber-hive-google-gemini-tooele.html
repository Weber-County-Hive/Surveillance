The Contract Nobody Voted On

USBE's Superintendent announced Google Gemini for 680,000 Utah students without a board vote, a privacy review, or a procurement process. The contract behind it lets Google expand its own reach by editing its own website.

On June 3, 2026, Utah State Board of Education Superintendent Molly Hart and AI Specialist Matt Winters announced that Google Gemini for Education would be made available across the state's public schools — roughly 680,000 K-12 students. The elected 15-member State Board of Education never voted on it. There was no procurement process. There was no privacy review. Eight days later, on June 11, Tooele County School District signed the underlying Data Processing Agreement with Google.

This investigation was built independently — through direct review of the signed contract, Utah's own student-privacy statutes, and a GRAMA records request to USBE itself. USBE's own written responses to that request confirm no documentation exists for three separate legal requirements that were supposed to happen before a rollout like this could occur.

Three requirements. Zero records.

Each of these comes from Utah Code, not from advocacy. USBE's own GRAMA response is the source for each "no documentation" finding.

§53G-7-228(2)(a)
evidence-based safety review before deployment
§53G-7-1402(2)(j)
written parental notification before generative AI use
§53E-9-309(2)(a)
specific audit & termination terms in the vendor contract

USBE's GRAMA response confirmed it holds no records showing any of the three steps above happened before, or at, the June 3 announcement. The third requirement — audit and termination provisions — is the exact statute the Legislature went on to strengthen later that same session with H.B. 55, discussed further down.

Worth being precise about Two of these three requirements come from H.B. 273, which did not take legal effect until July 1, 2026 — nearly a month after the June 3 announcement and three weeks after Tooele signed. That timing doesn't make the rollout look better: USBE knew the law was coming and moved ahead of it anyway, and the requirements clearly apply to any actual classroom deployment happening after July 1. But the announcement itself, narrowly, predates the two AI-specific laws being legally binding. The third requirement, on contract audit/termination terms, is not new to this year and applied throughout.

The Floor Amendment

The bill's own sponsor loosened the exact standard that would have excluded Gemini

H.B. 273 (Classroom Technology Amendments), the bill that eventually created the safety-review and parental-notification requirements above, was carried by Sen. Chris H. Wilson as Senate floor sponsor. The night before final Senate passage, Wilson introduced Floor Amendment 5, which changed the eligibility standard for classroom instructional technology from "designed specifically for instructional use" to "demonstrated to have significant educational value" — a broader, more subjective bar. The Utah Association of Public Charter Schools had separately warned that the original, narrower standard would exclude general-purpose tools like Google and Microsoft's products. Sen. Wilson received a contribution from Encode AI twelve days before H.B. 273 was introduced.

The same amendment also struck the bill's parent-transparency provisions — a list of digital tools in use, on-request access to a student's device and browsing history, and an opt-in alert for flagged content. The final enrolled law still contains all three. Which floor action restored them, and when, is not yet resolved.

Not the only AI infrastructure bill this session

USBE's own January 7, 2026 board minutes show the board weighing a separate AI vendor, "School AI," whose own funding request described replacing "education tools with a unified, state-governed AI infrastructure that supports students, teachers, and families through insights, shared data, and coordinated student support statewide." The board directed staff to work with the bill's sponsor to address concerns rather than oppose it outright — a motion to oppose failed 3-11. The same meeting saw the board direct staff to coordinate with Sen. Kirk Cullimore on a separate bill, S.B. 267, Software in Education — one of three EdTech-privacy bills Cullimore or his frequent co-sponsor John D. Johnson touched in the same session, alongside H.B. 55 and S.B. 322.

The state's own privacy office confirms, in writing, that none of the three legally required steps happened. The contract that resulted lets a single vendor expand what it collects and who it shares it with by editing its own website.
