The Safeguard Utah Killed, Playing Out 2,000 Miles Away

A Fort Myers man spent a night in jail for a crime committed 300 miles from home, after a facial-recognition system called him a 93% match. Utah had a bill that would have banned exactly this kind of technology fusion. It died in Senate Rules.

The Weber County Hive

In November 2023, someone tried to lure a child at a McDonald’s in Jacksonville Beach, Florida. A Jacksonville Beach detective photographed a computer screen showing grainy surveillance footage with his own cellphone, then submitted those photos to FACES — the Face Analysis Comparison and Examination System, a facial-recognition tool operated by the Pinellas County Sheriff’s Office and shared out to other Florida agencies. The system returned a candidate it scored as a 93 percent match on facial features: Robert Dillon, a 52-year-old Fort Myers commercial crabber who had never set foot in Jacksonville Beach. That score measured algorithmic similarity between two images — it did not mean a 93 percent probability that Dillon was the person in the footage. Investigators treated it as the latter anyway.

Nine months later, a Lee County deputy showed up at Dillon’s home and arrested him. He spent a night in jail, had to pledge the title to his truck to post bond, and carried a public mugshot that outlasted the charges themselves. Prosecutors eventually dropped the case. No record of the arrest remains in Duval County’s jail files or court system. On June 10, 2026, Dillon filed a federal civil-rights lawsuit — Dillon v. City of Jacksonville Beach — against the Jacksonville Beach Police Department and the Jacksonville and Pinellas County Sheriff’s Offices, represented by the ACLU, the ACLU of Florida, and the law firm Hoguet Newman Regal & Kenney.

What the warrant left out

According to the lawsuit, the arrest warrant never mentioned two things investigators already knew: a license-plate-reader search had turned up no record of Dillon’s vehicle anywhere near the restaurant in the days around the crime, and the McDonald’s employee who identified him in a photo lineup had described the actual suspect as a “regular” — something Dillon, a man who lived five hours away, could not have been. The lineup itself was built entirely from the facial-recognition system’s own output, which is precisely the failure mode researchers have flagged for years: when the algorithm gets a match wrong, it tends to get it wrong with someone who looks similar to the real suspect, so the lineup ends up testing whether a witness can tell two similar-looking strangers apart, not whether they recognize the person they actually saw.

Pinellas County’s own position, stated publicly after the lawsuit was filed, is that FACES results are never delivered as identifications — that the tool is explicitly meant to generate investigative leads requiring independent corroboration, not probable cause on its own. Whether that standard was followed here is the entire dispute. What isn’t disputed is the outcome: a man with no connection to the crime, its city, or its victim spent a night in jail and is still dealing with the fallout two years later.

Dillon isn't an outlier

Civil-liberties advocates say Dillon became at least the 15th publicly identified person wrongfully arrested after police relied on an incorrect facial-recognition result — that count reflects the ACLU's own April 2026 chronology, which listed Maryland grandmother Kimberlee Williams as the 14th known case shortly before Dillon's became public. The national total varies depending on whether a given count includes arrests, prolonged detentions, withdrawn warrants, and still-disputed cases; News4Jax separately reported attorneys' broader claim of more than two dozen people, which is their own figure, not an independently confirmed one. Why This Belongs In Utah's File

H.B. 468 would have banned exactly this fusion

Utah’s H.B. 468, Automatic License Plate Reader Amendments, passed House Transportation 10–0–2 and the House floor 64–2 in 2025. Among its provisions: an explicit ban on attaching facial recognition technology to ALPR hardware statewide. The bill cleared Senate Transportation 6–0–2 as well — every committee that heard it, in both chambers, voted for it. It never got a Senate floor vote. Its enacting clause was struck at 11:59 p.m. on March 7, 2025, the same night and the same mechanism that killed S.B. 138, a companion bill that never even got a hearing.

Nothing in Utah’s current ALPR statute prohibits combining plate-reader networks with face-matching systems the way Florida’s patchwork of shared facial-recognition databases now does. The safeguard existed, on paper, with unanimous committee support in both chambers. It didn’t survive Rules.

The dates don’t line up as cause and effect — Utah’s bill couldn’t have prevented an arrest in Florida. What lines up is the shape of the risk. A state legislature identified the specific technological fusion that makes cases like Dillon’s possible, wrote a rule against it, and watched every committee that reviewed it vote in favor before it disappeared in the one room this series keeps finding at the end of every dead surveillance bill.

Every committee with jurisdiction voted for the safeguard. None of that mattered once the bill reached Rules with the session's final days closing in — the same sentence this series has now written about ALPR reform three times.
