The Camera Said "Match." Police Drew Their Guns.

Flock alerts and vehicle-location records have helped police solve real crimes. They have also put innocent people at gunpoint. "Flock was wrong" isn't always technically accurate — sometimes the camera worked exactly as designed, and something else in the chain failed instead.

What an alert actually establishes — and what it doesn't

Before the two cases below, it's worth being precise about what a Flock match actually is.

An alert can establish:

  • A camera recorded a plate resembling a target plate
  • At a particular time
  • At a particular location
  • Associated with a database entry

It does not, by itself, establish:

  • Who was driving
  • Whether the database entry is still valid
  • Whether the plate was cloned
  • Whether the vehicle owner committed a crime
  • Whether the observation supplies reasonable suspicion or probable cause on its own

Case 1

Amber Newell: stopped at gunpoint twice in one week

Brookfield, Wisconsin — August 6, 2026. The camera worked. The alert behind it didn't.

AN

Amber Newell

I-94, Brookfield, WI — Aug. 6, 2026, ~4:45 PM

"I haven't been asleep since this has happened. Every time I close my eyes, all I can see is guns."
— Amber Newell, to FOX6 Milwaukee
What happened: Video from I-94 showed multiple officers with guns drawn and a passenger's hands out the window. Brookfield officers contacted the Milwaukee Police Department to verify the homicide alert — MPD said the vehicle and its occupants were no longer of interest, and Newell and her passengers were released. This was the second time that week: Newell says she was stopped at gunpoint in Milwaukee days earlier, towed, and released with no explanation. Milwaukee police confirmed staff had failed to remove the wanted-vehicle alert from the system after the investigation no longer needed it — calling it a personnel issue, "not a Flock issue." Brookfield Police Chief Christopher Garcia defended the stop, saying his officers "clearly had sufficient information and objective facts to support taking the actions they did." Milwaukee Police Chief Jeffrey Norman has separately defended the technology generally. Flock told FOX6 its readers capture images in public view, don't use facial recognition, require documented search reasons, and create permanent audit records — all true statements that don't address why a cleared alert stayed live.
Case 2

Chrisanna Elser: "it is a lock, there is zero doubt"

Bow Mar / Fort Logan, Colorado — September 27, 2025. The camera worked here too. What failed was the conclusion an officer drew from it.

CE

Chrisanna Elser

Fort Logan, Denver, CO — Sept. 27, 2025

"You know we have cameras in that jurisdiction and you can't get a breath of fresh air, in or out of that place, without us knowing, correct? ... Just as an example, you've driven there about 20 times in the last month."
— Sgt. Jamie Milliman, Columbine Valley Police Dept., per Elser's doorbell camera (Colorado Sun)
What happened: Flock cameras in the town of Bow Mar had captured Elser's forest-green Rivian passing through between 11:52 a.m. and 12:09 p.m. on the day a $25 package was stolen from a porch. Sgt. Jamie Milliman told her, "It is locked in. There is zero doubt. I wouldn't have come here unless I was 100% sure," and handed her a court summons — without showing her the Flock footage or the doorbell video he claimed showed her stealing the package, and without reviewing the dashcam footage from her own Rivian that showed her exact route. She had, in fact, driven through Bow Mar that day — to a tailor appointment, more than a quarter mile from the theft the entire time. Weeks of her own evidence-gathering (tailor surveillance footage, her truck's onboard cameras, GPS logs) got the summons voided by the police chief, who wrote her: "After reviewing the evidence you have provided (nicely done btw), we have voided the summons." Milliman was later reprimanded for "rude" and "dismissive" behavior and ordered to complete de-escalation, community-relations, and interviewing training within 30 days.
What Officers Are Supposed To Do

The verification step that's supposed to sit between an alert and a stop

Weber County's own current ALPR policy text wasn't found published online in this search — a direct GRAMA request is the way to confirm its exact language. What follows is the near-identical Lexipol template language used by Utah County Sheriff's Office, Emery County Sheriff's Office, Murray PD, and Nephi PD — all under the same statute, §41-6a-2003, that governs Weber County.

  • —"An ALPR may be used in conjunction with any routine patrol operation or official investigation. Reasonable suspicion or probable cause is not required before using an ALPR." — standard language across every Utah agency policy found in this research
  • —Utah Code §41-6a-2003 limits WHEN an ALPR can be used (active investigation, outstanding warrant, missing person, stolen vehicle) — it does not require verification before officers act on what the system returns
  • ✓"If practicable, the deputy should verify an ALPR response through the appropriate law enforcement database before taking enforcement action that is based solely upon an ALPR alert." — Utah County Sheriff's Office Policy 426(e), identical language in Utah DPS Policy 639
  • —"If practicable" is doing real work in that sentence — it is a should, conditioned on practicality, not a shall
  • —No Utah policy found in this research requires dispatch to independently reconfirm an alert's currency before officers are dispatched to a stop
  • —No policy found requires a second high-risk stop on the same person to trigger automatic review — which is exactly what happened to Newell
The Timing Problem

Why audits don't prevent the encounter

Utah requires a 5-year audit record of every search — crime type and incident number included, per §41-6a-2005. That record answers "who searched, and why" after the fact. It does the driver at gunpoint no good in the moment.

1Alert issued
2Officer receives it
3Stop occurs
4Person detained
5Report completed
6Audit, maybe, later

The audit trail can establish responsibility after the fact. It is not built to stop officers from acting on bad information in step 2.

The Throughline

Neither camera misread a plate. In Newell's case, an accurate camera fed a stale alert into a network with no automatic check on whether a cleared vehicle was still live. In Elser's case, an accurate location observation became a human conclusion nobody verified before a summons was issued. Different failures, same lesson: the technology can work exactly as designed and someone can still end up at gunpoint, or in court, over nothing.
