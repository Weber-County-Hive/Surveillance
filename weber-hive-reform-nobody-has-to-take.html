The Reform Nobody Has to Take

On August 13, 2026, Flock announced a sweeping set of privacy reforms for its ALPR network — new retention defaults, mandatory case codes, automated misuse detection. Read closely, one of the headline changes is optional. Nothing in the announcement requires any agency to actually adopt it.

What Flock Actually Announced

A real reform package, confirmed industry-wide

This is not in dispute — Flock's own blog post and more than a dozen news outlets confirm the same set of changes, announced August 13, 2026.

  • A new 7-day default retention recommendation, down from 30 days, with a new “Evidence Mode” that lets detectives preserve specific records tied to an active investigation past that window
  • Mandatory case codes — officers must enter a code from their agency's own records management system before running a search, required for all law enforcement customers by January 1, 2027
  • Mandatory Audit Assistance — a machine-learning tool that flags abnormal search patterns for administrator review, optional since April 2026, becoming mandatory for all law enforcement customers by the end of 2026
  • Proactive lockouts — an account whose activity meets Flock's criteria for abnormal behavior automatically loses access pending administrator review
  • Offense-type filtering — a customer agency can now choose which categories of offense (homicide, arson, etc.) outside agencies are allowed to search its data for, which Flock says would let an agency block outside searches tied to immigration enforcement specifically
  • Security changes responsive to a November 2025 letter from Sen. Ron Wyden to the FTC flagging the lack of mandatory multi-factor authentication: Flock is now requiring MFA, commissioning an independent security review by Bishop Fox, and launching a Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure program
The Detail That Matters

Not every reform in this package works the same way

Toggle between the two categories below. This is the single most important distinction in the whole announcement.

  • ✓Case codes required before a search runs — applies to all law enforcement customers by Jan. 1, 2027
  • ✓Audit Assistance activated — applies to all law enforcement customers by end of 2026
  • ✓Proactive lockout on flagged abnormal activity — tied to Audit Assistance, same mandatory rollout
  • ✓Multi-factor authentication — security requirement, applies broadly per Flock's announcement

No opt-in, no change. That's the whole loophole in one sentence.

  • ₀7-day default retention — new customers get this as the default; existing customers keep their current, previously approved retention period unless they choose to switch
  • ₀Offense-type filtering for outside searches — a capability an agency can choose to configure, not something applied automatically

Nothing in the public reporting on this announcement describes a mechanism for confirming which agencies have opted in to the shorter retention window and which haven't, or a public list of which posture each of Flock's thousands of law enforcement customers has chosen.

Two Paths, Same Announcement

What "Flock announced shorter retention" can actually mean for a given agency

Click each path. Both are fully consistent with the same headline.

Path A

Agency opts in

Switches to the new 7-day default, gains access to Evidence Mode for active-investigation exceptions.

Path B

Agency does nothing

Keeps whatever retention period it already had — 30 days, 60 days, or longer — with no requirement to announce that choice publicly.

Path A, in practice: The agency's retention window shortens to 7 days for ordinary captures. Detectives can still preserve specific records tied to an active case using Evidence Mode, so nothing about actual investigations is lost — only the default window for data nobody has flagged as relevant to anything narrows.
Path B, in practice: Nothing changes. The agency's retention period is whatever it was before August 13 — which, per multiple Flock ALPR audits reviewed elsewhere in this series, is very commonly still the original 30-day manufacturer default. The agency can describe itself as covered by Flock's "reformed" platform without having adopted the one reform that actually shortens how long ordinary location data is kept.
The Bigger Gap Behind The Opt-In

Utah's rules stop applying the moment the data leaves Utah

The opt-in question above is about what Flock recommends as a company default. It's a smaller question than this one: once Weber County's data is searched and pulled into another agency's own system, whose rules govern it from then on? For Weber County's single largest out-of-state searcher, the honest answer is: almost none.

Utah has a real statutory floor. Texas doesn't have one at all.

Utah's SB 250 framework — documented in Article 4 of this series — caps Weber County's own retention at 9 months, requires a 5-year audit trail, and restricts permitted uses to specific categories. Texas has none of that. As of this writing, The Texan reported plainly that in Texas "there are no state or federal rules limiting how the data is used or how long the vendor may retain collected information." Two bills that would have changed that — HB 961 and HB 3999 — were introduced in the Texas Legislature and did not pass.

Houston PD's own retention claims don't agree with each other

A Houston City Council member told KHOU in 2024 that Flock data "can only be kept for 30 days." But Houston Police Department's own written policy — General Order 400-29, issued August 2024 — says the opposite for exactly this kind of data: non-city-owned ALPR systems (which is what Flock is, from Houston's perspective) are to be purged "within one calendar year of entry unless a law enforcement agency requests a longer retention period for a specific case." A public statement promising 30 days and a written department policy allowing up to a year are not the same rule, and nothing found in this research resolves which one actually governs.

This isn't just Houston. It's most of the network as it already exists.

Follow the opt-in structure to its actual conclusion: the new 7-day default applies automatically to new Flock customers. Every agency already running Flock before August 13, 2026 keeps its own previously approved retention period unless it takes affirmative action to switch. This series has already documented what those pre-existing periods commonly are — 30 days as Flock's own prior company default at agency after agency in independent state audits, Utah's separate 9-month statutory ceiling, and Houston's own written policy allowing up to a year. None of those change because of this announcement unless the specific agency holding that data chooses to act. The "30 days down to 7" headline describes what a brand-new customer signing up today gets. For the thousands of agencies — and the roughly 5,000+ communities Flock says it already operates in — already running the system, the retention window announced August 13 is not a change at all unless each one separately opts in.

What this means for a Weber County resident's data, concretely:

  • Utah law caps how long Weber County's own copy of a capture can be kept, and requires a 5-year record of who searched it and why
  • The moment Houston PD searches and pulls that data into its own system, Utah's cap and audit requirement no longer apply to that copy
  • What governs Houston's copy from that point is Houston's own internal policy — which Houston alone can write, interpret, or change, and which its own public statements and its own written order don't fully agree on
  • Texas's statewide ALPR-sharing network has grown from 122 to 206 partner agencies in five months as of August 2026, per the Texas Tribune, with no state law setting any floor under any of them
One Layer Up

Could an agency even be legally free to keep 7 days if it wanted to?

On the plate images themselves

Yes. No federal statute sets any ALPR retention length at all — the Congressional Research Service's own review confirms there are "no federal laws that explicitly govern or limit the use of ALPR technology... by law enforcement agencies." Most state laws found in this research (Utah's 9-month cap, Colorado's 3-year cap, California's 60-day cap, New Jersey's 5-year allowance) set a maximum, not a minimum — a ceiling an agency can always choose to fall well under. Nothing in that layer blocks an agency from adopting 7 days if it chose to.

On the access logs — a separate federal layer entirely

The FBI's CJIS Security Policy binds any agency with access to NCIC or other Criminal Justice Information — effectively every agency whose ALPR system checks plates against a hotlist. CJIS names "Audit Record Retention" as one of its required policy areas, and multiple secondary sources describing that policy cite a minimum one-year retention requirement for logs of who accessed CJI and when. That's a different record than the plate capture — it's the access log, not the image — but it means an agency could delete the raw plate photo after 7 days and still be separately required, by a federal security policy with nothing to do with Flock's announcement, to keep the record of who searched for it far longer.

This series has not obtained the primary CJIS Security Policy text confirming the exact retention clause and version in force. Flagged here as a real, credibly-sourced possibility — not a confirmed requirement. Worth verifying directly against CJIS documentation before treating it as settled.

This is the same shape as the opt-in retention question above, at a larger scale: a real rule exists somewhere in the chain, but it only covers part of the chain, and nothing requires the rest of the chain to match it.

How Critics Read the Same Announcement

"Window dressing," or "a step in the right direction"?

Both reactions are on the record, from different vantage points.

ACLU
Called the shorter retention period "a step in the right direction" — while dismissing the other safeguards as "retreads" of inadequate protections.
Robert Frommer, Institute for Justice
Called the reforms "window dressing" from a company in "panic mode," arguing searches should instead require approval "by judges with real warrants."

The gap that survives the reform, per outside analysis

Even after case codes become mandatory in January 2027, nothing in the announced changes verifies that a code an officer enters actually corresponds to a real, open case. An officer can still type any case number into the required field. The mandatory field closes the "no reason given at all" gap this series has documented elsewhere — it does not close the "nobody checks if the reason is true" gap.

The Throughline

This is the same shape as every legislative reform this series has documented — real, mandatory pieces (case codes, audit tools, MFA) sitting next to an optional piece (the actual retention shortening) that gets described in headlines as if it applies to everyone. It doesn't. It applies to agencies signing up today. Every agency already running Flock — including, as far as this piece can confirm, Weber County's own — keeps whatever it already had unless it separately chooses to change. Whether Weber County has taken that step isn't something this piece can answer. It's the next records request.
