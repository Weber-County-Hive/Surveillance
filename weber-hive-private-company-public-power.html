Private Company, Public Power

Flock is a private vendor. The cameras run on public power, watch public roads, and feed a network searched by public employees. When something goes wrong — a stale alert, a data breach, an unauthorized search — the honest answer to "whose fault is that" is often: it depends which link in the chain you ask.

The Legal Starting Point

Flock is a government contractor. That status does most of the work.

Two separate statutes decide how much of this is Weber County's responsibility and how much is Flock's — and both turn on the same word: contractor.

S.B. 250's government-agent framing

Utah's ALPR statute treats a vendor operating under contract with a law enforcement agency as effectively standing in the agency's shoes for purposes of the statute's restrictions and permitted-use rules — the obligations attach to the government's use of the system, with the vendor bound by the terms of its contract rather than by an independent statutory duty of its own.

H.B. 450's contractor requirements

A contractor that processes or has access to personal data under a contract with a governmental entity is subject to the Government Data Privacy Act "to the same extent as the governmental entity." But contracts entered into or renewed only have to contain that compliance language starting July 1, 2027 — pushed back a year from the original bill's July 1, 2026 date. Weber County's Flock contract, signed in 2021, isn't required to include this language until its next renewal after that date.

Where This Exemption Came From

The 2022 law nobody debated as a surveillance question

S.B. 227 (2022) — the government-contractor exemption

The Utah Consumer Privacy Act exempts a person acting as a government contractor from most of its consumer-data obligations. That exemption was in S.B. 227 as introduced by Sen. Kirk Cullimore, passed 28–0 in the Senate and 71–1 in the House, and signed by Gov. Cox on March 24, 2022. Nothing in that record shows the Legislature debating how the exemption would apply to a company like Flock, which uses government contracts to operate a nationwide surveillance and vehicle-recognition network. The exemption was written for ordinary state contractors — software vendors, printers, consultants — not with ALPR specifically in mind, and it has applied to ALPR vendors since.

The Throughline

A private company operates infrastructure that exercises public power — and the law splits responsibility for it three or four ways at once. Ask Flock who's accountable and they can point to the county's contract. Ask the county and they can point to Flock's technology. Ask the state and they can point to a statute written for government contractors in general, never surveillance vendors specifically. Every answer is partly true. None of them is complete.
